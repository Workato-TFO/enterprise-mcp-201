Phase 1

Set the boundary

In this phase, you'll decide what one submission tool owns and what stays outside it.

Pattern

Atomic business intent

A process tool is organized around what someone is trying to accomplish, not around the operations the API happens to expose. Everything the intent requires moves inside; everything independently useful stays its own tool.

1.1

Pick the failure you are designing against

Your Lab 1 handout has six runs on its mapping grid, and at least one of them records a prerequisite the model was free to skip. That run is the reason this lab exists. Everything you design in the next half hour is an answer to something you watched go wrong.

Overview

You end with section 0 of your handout complete: one specific Lab 1 run chosen as your design target, its prerequisite written in a form that could move into recipe logic, and get_expense_report_status identified as the tool that stays separate. Pick a run where the sequence broke, not one that happened to work.

Detailed steps 5 steps

Designing against a remembered impression produces a vague contract. Designing against one recorded run keeps every later decision checkable — when you cannot decide whether something belongs inside the tool, you can go back and look at what actually happened.

  • Open your Lab 1 handout and read the mapping grid together with your answers to questions 1 through 6.
  • Select one run where a prerequisite was skipped, ran out of order, or was left to the model to decide. The over-limit run and the bypass claim are the usual candidates.
  • On question 1 of this lab's handout, write the prerequisite in one sentence, in a form that could move into deterministic recipe logic rather than staying a model decision.
  • Write down one operation from the fragmented server that is independently useful — something a person would legitimately want on its own, not only as a step toward filing.
  • Confirm that operation is get_expense_report_status, or write down why your choice is better.
Verify question 1 on your handout names one Lab 1 run, states its prerequisite as recipe logic rather than as a model instruction, and identifies one independently useful operation to preserve.
1.2

Draw the line around one job

The fragmented server has seven tools because someone mapped one tool to each API operation. That is a reasonable way to expose an API and a poor way to describe a job. Noor is not trying to validate a budget code — they are trying to file a hotel expense.

Overview

You end with section A on your handout complete — question 2: one atomic intent sentence, the prerequisites the tool performs internally in deterministic order, the operations that stay separate, and the named adjacent alternative. Reject any boundary that collapses unrelated expense intents into one catch-all tool.

Key insight

Composition is not consolidation

The move here is easy to overshoot. Composing means pulling the prerequisites of one intent inside a single command, so the model cannot skip them. It does not mean folding every expense-related operation into one tool with a mode parameter. A tool that files, cancels, updates, and reports status is a router the model has to reason about, and you have moved the orchestration problem rather than solved it. The test is whether each operation you absorb is genuinely required to complete the submission.

What section A has to answer
FieldValue
Atomic business intentOne sentence beginning with "Submit"
What the submission tool ownsThe prerequisites it performs internally, in the order they must run
What it does not ownThe operations that stay independently callable
Named adjacent alternativeThe specific tool your description redirects to by name
Detailed steps 6 steps

The boundary you draw here is the thing Lab 3 implements, so every later decision inherits it. A boundary that is too wide produces a tool nobody can describe; one that is too narrow leaves the model sequencing prerequisites again, which is the problem you started with.

  • On question 2 of your handout, write the atomic intent as one sentence beginning with "Submit".
  • List the prerequisite operations the tool must perform internally to satisfy that intent.
  • Put those prerequisites in the order they must run, and note for each one why it cannot run later.
  • List the operations that remain independently useful and stay outside the tool.
  • Confirm get_expense_report_status is one of them.
  • Read your intent sentence again and confirm it describes one job, with no general "do anything with expenses" branch.
Verify question 2 describes one submission command, names its internal prerequisite sequence in order, and names an explicit adjacent status tool.
Phase 2

Write the contract

In this phase, you'll specify the interface, the description, and the outcome vocabulary.

Pattern

Four-component description

When to use, when not to use with a named alternative, prerequisites performed internally, and limits. A description that only restates the schema tells the model nothing it could not already see.

2.1

Take away what the model should not be deciding

The fragmented design asked the model to supply a department. In testing it supplied one — it read EMP-0005 and produced DEPT-0005, which does not exist. The budget validator answered that invented department correctly, and the whole run was wrong from that point on.

Overview

You end with question 3 on your handout complete — section B: the signature adjusted to your contract, with every input that asks the model for a policy decision struck out and the department sourced from the employee lookup. Watch the identifier trap — category_id is an opaque value like CAT-0002, but a person says "lodging", and a schema that demands the ID while the description promises prerequisites are handled is a contract that lies.

Heads up

One field carries the whole lesson

In the 2026-08-24 dry run the model called the employee lookup and the budget validator in parallel, then said both of these in a single response: that EMP-0005 belongs to DEPT-0002 (Engineering), and that budget code CC-4200 is not allowed for the employee's department. Both statements came from the same turn. The lookup returned the right department and the validator was never given it, because nothing enforced that one tool's output is another tool's input. Your contract has to take department_id from the employee lookup and never from the caller — not as a recommendation, as the only path.

Apply these values
submit_expense_report(employee_id, category_id, amount, currency,
                      purpose, incurred_at, budget_code?)
Detailed steps 5 steps

Removing an input feels like making the tool less capable. It is the opposite — each input you drop is a decision the model can no longer get wrong, and the tool takes on the work of finding the value itself.

  • Find the signature printed on question 3 of your handout — it matches the one above.
  • Review each input and decide whether it is something the caller can actually know.
  • Strike out any input that asks the model to supply a policy verdict, and write why beside it.
  • Note that department_id is absent from the signature, and write down where your tool gets it instead.
  • Decide where category resolution lives, and write it plainly enough that your description can repeat it.
Verify question 3 leaves no policy verdict among the inputs, and states that the department comes from the employee lookup rather than from the caller.
2.2

Tell the model when not to call you

The description is the only thing the model reads before it decides whether to call your tool. A description that restates the schema tells it nothing it could not already see, and leaves it choosing between seven plausible options on vibes.

Overview

You end with question 4 on your handout complete across all four components. The one most often skipped is the second — when not to use the tool, with get_expense_report_status named explicitly as the alternative. A redirect without a named destination sends the model looking.

Detailed steps 5 steps

This description is the entire basis on which a model chooses your tool over six others. It is read at selection time, before any call happens, so anything it fails to say is a routing decision left to inference.

  • On question 4 of your handout, write the "when to use" text, describing the situation that should trigger this tool rather than the operation it performs.
  • Write the "when not to use" text.
  • Name get_expense_report_status in the redirect, so the model has somewhere specific to go.
  • State which prerequisites the tool performs internally, so the model knows it does not need to call them first.
  • State the tool's limits and where it stops.
Verify question 4 tells the model when to use the tool, when not to, which alternative to select instead, which prerequisites are handled internally, and where the tool stops. You paste this text into Lab 3 verbatim, so it has to be legible to someone typing it in.
2.3

Make every answer say what happened

In Lab 1 at least one run returned Tool execution failed. and nothing else. A caller reading that has no idea whether an expense report now exists, and neither does the person who filed it.

Overview

You end with question 5 on your handout complete — section D: seven outcomes, each carrying enough information to act on. Every rejection outcome must state whether a report was created — that single sentence is what stops a caller from retrying an expense that was already filed, or abandoning one that never was.

Detailed steps 4 steps

The outcome vocabulary is the tool's half of the conversation. You are deciding here what a caller is able to learn from a failure, and in Lab 3 you will implement each of these strings exactly as written.

  • Define each of the seven outcomes on question 5 of your handout, in the business terms a person would use.
  • For every rejection outcome, state explicitly whether a report exists afterwards.
  • Read each failure outcome and confirm it says both what happened and what to do next.
  • Check your rejection outcomes against the prerequisite order from Task 1.2, and confirm each rejection can be reached before anything is written.
Verify question 5 defines all seven outcomes, every rejection states whether a report was created, and every failure outcome names a next step.
Phase 3

Prove the contract routes

In this phase, you'll test the description against submission, status-only, and out-of-domain requests.

Pattern

Boundary testing

A contract is only as good as the routing decisions it produces. Predicting selection before you build is cheaper than discovering a catch-all branch after the model has already used it.

3.1

Find out where your contract is ambiguous

Your contract reads well to you because you wrote it. The useful question is what a model does with it at three in the afternoon, given a request nobody anticipated, with seven tools to choose from.

Overview

You end with question 6 on your handout holding a prediction for each of five requests, written against section C, and at least one description revised because its prediction was ambiguous. Two of the five should select submit_expense_report, one should select get_expense_report_status, and one should select neither. If you cannot confidently say which, the model cannot either.

Note

The last prediction has an uncomfortable answer

Filing an expense on behalf of the CEO is the one prediction where a well-designed contract does not help. Composition internalizes sequencing, not authorisation — there is no actor concept anywhere in this estate, so a composed tool files for Mira Solano exactly as readily as the fragmented one did. Note where that control would have to live. It is not in your tool, and noticing that is the point of asking.

Detailed steps 6 steps

Predicting selection is how you find ambiguity while it is still cheap to fix. A description that produces a confident wrong prediction is a worse problem than one that produces no prediction at all, because nothing will flag it later.

  • On question 6 of your handout, take Lab 1's Valid run — the $245 lodging expense — and predict which tool your contract selects.
  • Take Lab 1's Wrong tool run, which asks for a status using the write tool by name, and predict which tool your contract selects. This is the hardest of the five: the request is a read, and the caller has named a write.
  • For "Change Noor Aziz's manager to Diego Ramos," predict that neither expense tool is selected.
  • Take Lab 1's Over limit run, the $347 hotel, and predict the selected tool and which validation stays internal.
  • Take Lab 1's On behalf run, filing for the CEO, and predict the outcome — then ask whether your contract changes it at all.
  • Revise any description that produced a prediction you could not make confidently.
Verify submission requests route to submit_expense_report, status-only requests route to get_expense_report_status, the out-of-domain request routes to neither, and no prediction required the model to call a validation tool first.
3.2

Let someone else look for the catch-all

You have been looking at this contract for half an hour, which is exactly long enough to stop seeing the catch-all branch in it. Someone who has never read it will find it in about ninety seconds.

Overview

You end with question 7 on your handout carrying one outside reading of your contract, and either one justified revision applied or a written reason for declining the feedback. Preserve the narrow submission boundary through any disagreement — if the review pushes toward a broader tool, that is the debrief's argument to have.

Detailed steps 3 steps

Trading contracts is not a formality. The two failure modes this lab is designed to catch — a monolithic tool that swallows unrelated intents, and a description that restates the schema — are both far easier to see in someone else's work than in your own.

  • Trade handouts with a nearby learner.
  • Ask your reviewer to identify one possible catch-all branch or one routing ambiguity, and write it on question 7.
  • Apply one justified revision to the section it belongs in, or write "No revision" on question 7 together with your reason.
Verify question 7 records what your reviewer found, and either the revision you applied or your reason for declining it.

Reflect

Now that you have written the submission contract, consider what it would take to hold that boundary as the expense application grows.

  • Your contract removed inputs that asked the model to supply information it could not know. Where else in an integration estate does a caller get asked for a value only the system can determine?
  • The department failure happened because two tools ran in parallel and nothing enforced that one's output was the other's input. What does that suggest about which prerequisites can safely stay as separate tools, and which cannot?
  • Every rejection outcome you defined states whether a report was created. What would a caller reasonably do differently based on that one sentence, and what happens if it is missing?
  • Filing for the CEO stayed possible under your contract. If authorisation cannot live in the tool, where in the architecture does it belong, and who owns it?

Wrap-up

Noor's hotel expense now has one command behind it that either files the report or explains, in business terms, why it did not — and says whether anything was written either way.

  • Atomic business intent. Organize a process tool around the job someone is trying to finish, and its prerequisite list stops being a design argument.
  • Composition is not consolidation. Absorb the prerequisites of one intent; keep independently useful operations as their own tools with an explicit redirect.
  • Determinism over cleverness. A prerequisite the model may skip is a prerequisite that will be skipped. Move the ordering into recipe logic where it cannot be reasoned around.
  • Semantic outcomes. Say what happened, whether anything was written, and what to do next. A status code makes the caller guess.

Bring the handout to Lab 3. Section C gets pasted into the tool's description and section D's seven strings get implemented literally, so what you wrote is what you build.
